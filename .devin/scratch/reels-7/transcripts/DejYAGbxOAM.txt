Interview question. Ten thousand bots are hammering your login endpoint. How do you stop them, without locking out real users? The obvious answer is to block them, but these bots come from thousands of different IPs and each one sends just a few requests. No single source looks suspicious. Worse, login is the most expensive thing your server does. Passwords are hashed with B-Crypt, which is deliberately slow, so attackers can't guess quickly, but that same slowness means every fake attempt burns real CPU. Enough of them, and the attack becomes an outage. And these aren't random guesses. Attackers use email and password pairs leaked from other sites, hoping people reuse them. Each account gets only one or two tries, so a limit of five attempts never triggers. Locking accounts doesn't help either. Now attackers can lock out real users on purpose. So the fix isn't to verify better. It's to decide earlier. Put a cheap risk check in front of the expensive one, and only spend CPU on requests that look real. Every client introduces itself when it opens a secure connection, which ciphers its supports, in what order, with which extensions. A real iPhone app and a Python script produce different fingerprints, even if both claim to be an iPhone. No single clue is enough, since determined bots can fake a fingerprint. So you combine them. The fingerprint, how many of this client's attempts fail, and whether the device has been seen before. Low risk goes straight through. Medium risk gets a capture. High risk is dropped before it ever touches B-Crypt. Real users barely notice, and the bots get expensive. To sum up, don't just block, don't just lock out. Score each request cheaply, challenge the doubtful ones, and protect the slow, expensive step behind it. That's your interview answer.
